PYAAR

The War for the Heart

A Player's Manual · First Edition

Foreword

· · ·

PYAAR is a small game. It is played on a board of forty-nine squares, with eight pieces a side, in five to fifteen minutes. The rules fit on a single page. A new player understands the objective in twenty seconds.

What it asks of its players, though, is something a chess player will recognise. You will arrange your pieces. You will think about your opponent. You will see, three or four moves ahead, the shape that you and they are both reaching toward. One of you will reach it first.

The picture you are reaching toward is called The Embrace: your Lover at the centre of the board, two of your other pieces beside her. The Lover cannot be captured by enemies; she is the only piece in the game with that protection, and she is the only piece that can win. The other seven move and fight as their names suggest — Warrior, Sage, Traitor, Fool, Guardian, Dreamer, and the slow stubborn Soldier.

This is the manual for that game. It is short, but every section is here because it matters. Read it through once before your first game; read the strategy page again after your third.

— THE DESIGNER —

The Premise

Two armies on opposite shores. Between them, at the centre of the board, glows the Heart. Among each side's pieces is one who walks only toward that centre — the Lover. She cannot be touched. She cannot be removed. The opponent may block her path, deny her companions, trap her in a corner — but they may never strike her down.

The contest is not "kill her before she gets there." The contest is: can you bring her to the Heart with two friends at her side?

Why this game?

PYAAR is a chess-family game built around a single mechanic that doesn't exist anywhere else: the goal piece cannot be captured. In chess, the King is the most-protected piece because the rules forbid him from stepping into harm. Here, the Lover is the most-protected piece because nothing can harm her at all.

What this changes: the war is no longer about exterminating the enemy or chasing a king into a corner. It is a positional contest — you push your slow, sacred Lover toward the centre while clearing companions to meet her there, and your opponent does the same in the opposite direction. Whoever forms the picture first wins.

The game's name is a Hindi word for love. The pieces are archetypes — the universal cast of a love story made into a war: the lover, the warrior, the sage, the traitor, the fool, the guardian, the dreamer, and the soldier who pushes forward and never retreats.

The Game in One Sentence

Bring your Lover to the Heart at the centre of the board with at least two of your other pieces in the ring of cells around her — that is the Embrace, and that is the win.

The Board

A board of 49 cells in a 7 × 7 grid. The centre cell is The Heart. Each player begins with eight pieces — seven named, one Soldier.

Player 1's seven named pieces — Lover, Warrior, Sage, Fool, Traitor, Guardian, Dreamer — line the back rank. Their Soldier stands one cell forward, in the centre column. Player 2 mirrors them across the board.

The order along the back rank is not fixed — each player decides their own arrangement before play begins (see the next page).

The Setup

Before the first move, each player arranges their seven named pieces along their home row in any order they like. This is not chess. A row that is right for you against one opponent may be wrong against another. The arrangement is the first decision of the game, and it shapes every move that follows.

How it works

  1. Player 1 arranges first. The phase banner reads "Player 1 — arrange your characters."
  2. Click any two of your back-row pieces — they swap cells. Repeat as often as you like.
  3. Use Shuffle Mine to randomise your row instantly, if you want a fresh layout.
  4. When you are ready, press Begin Game.
  5. In vs Computer, Player 2's row is shuffled randomly when you confirm.
    In Hot-seat, Player 2 then arranges their row the same way, and presses Begin.

The Soldier is not arrangeable — he always begins at (2, d) for Player 1 and (6, d) for Player 2, one cell in front of the back row.

The Lover is your crown-piece — the only piece that can win the game. Place her where you want her to start her journey to the Heart. The other six combat pieces are her escorts, blockers, and bodyguards.

Three example arrangements

What makes a row good?

The Sage is a bishop — it moves only on diagonals. From a light cell it reaches only light cells; from a dark cell, only dark. Placing it on a particular colour commits it to half the board. Some players keep two corners covered by colour; others put the Sage where it threatens the d-file diagonal on move 1.

The Traitor is a rook. Wherever you put it, it sees one full row and one full column. The d-column is the path to the Heart — a Traitor stationed near d sees that file from turn 1, but is also the first target.

The Lover only walks toward the Heart. She is your slowest forward piece. Many players hide her behind the Guardian, so an enemy must trade first before reaching her.

The Dreamer cannot capture, but moves three cells in a straight line. She is your scout and your blocker — useful at the edge where she will not be in the way of your own attacks.

The Fool jumps. Where you place him barely matters in turn 1, but he likes to start one cell away from the central file so his L-jumps reach the enemy's middle in one move.

The Guardian and Warrior are short-range. Place them where you want them to fight — likely between your weak pieces (Lover, Dreamer) and the centre.

The Mind Game

In Hot-seat play, Player 1 commits first — Player 2 sees that arrangement before choosing their own. This is a real asymmetry: Player 2 can respond to the layout opposite them. Many players treat setup as a small contest of its own: hide your fragile pieces, threaten the lines your opponent has left open, and reserve your long-range pieces for where the enemy is weakest.

The Pieces

Each piece has exactly one movement pattern. Capture is by displacement: move onto an enemy, the enemy is exiled, you take their cell. No menus, no choices — click your piece, click a glowing cell, the move is done.

The Lover is Sacred

Of all eight pieces, only the Lover can win the game — and only the Lover cannot be captured. Enemy pieces may not move onto her cell. They may block her path, deny her companions, or push her into a corner — but they may not remove her. The seven other pieces fight to clear her way; she walks it.

How to Win · The Embrace

There is one positive way to win in PYAAR, and it is a picture. At the start of your turn, both must be true:

  1. Your Lover stands on the Heart at (d, 4).
  2. At least 2 of your other pieces stand on the eight cells of the Lotus — the ring of cells immediately around the Heart.

The "start of your turn" requirement means the opponent had one full move to disrupt — to push a piece into the Lotus to block, or to displace an escort. You held the formation through one storm.

The Lover is sacred

In every other piece-and-board game in this family (chess, Hnefatafl, Shogi), the goal piece is also a piece you protect from capture. In PYAAR, the Lover cannot be captured. Enemy pieces may not move onto her cell. They may block her, trap her, fight her companions — but they may never remove her. The contest is positional, not exterminative.

⚔ The Siege — the secondary path

If, at the start of your opponent's turn, they have no legal move, you win by The Siege. By the nature of PYAAR this almost always means their combat pieces have all been captured and their Lover stands alone, surrounded by your army, with no legal move. The opposing army has been crushed in the field.

The Siege is the secondary win. It happens when one side simply wipes the other off the board. A real victory — but the Embrace is the picture.

The 100-turn limit

If after 100 moves neither side has formed an Embrace or a Siege, the game ends with the side closer to the Heart winning the tiebreaker. Lover-distance is checked first, then escort count. In practice this rule almost never fires.

How to Play · The Turn

Once both players have arranged their back rank (see The Setup) the game begins. A turn is then two clicks. There are no menus, no decisions about how a piece moves — each piece has one rule. You only decide where.

The Computer

By default, Player 2 is the computer. The AI evaluates every legal move on its turn, scores positions by Lover-distance, Lotus presence, and material, and picks the strongest reply. It will push its Lover toward the centre relentlessly — your job is to get there first, or to plant blockers in its Lotus before it can land.

A button in the side panel toggles vs Computer / Hot-seat (two human players sharing one screen).

The Side Panel

  • Turn — which player is to move, and the move count.
  • Roster — total pieces alive per side.
  • New Game / Flip Board / Rules — controls.

Strategy

The Setup

Your arrangement is a turn-zero decision. The d-column is the natural road for your Lover toward the Heart, so where she starts on row 1 matters — putting her on the centre file (d1) gives the shortest straight march, but it's also the file the opponent will most expect. Putting her at b1 or f1 lets her slide diagonally toward the Heart through less contested cells. Place your long-range pieces where they can threaten the Lotus on move 1.

The Opening

Push early. The Lover is your goal piece — move her every turn that it's safe to do so. She is uncapturable but slow (1 cell, only toward the Heart), so each turn she stands still is a turn the opponent gains on you.

Send your fastest piece to the Lotus early — usually a Dreamer (3 cells in a line) or a Fool (knight L-jumps). Getting any piece into the enemy's Lotus is denial: if the opponent's Lotus is occupied by your blocker, they need to capture you out of it.

Keep your Soldier in reserve. He moves slowly and only forward — most useful as a Lotus body once the d-column has been cleared.

The Middle Game

Captures matter, but not the way they do in chess. You are not trying to wipe the board — you are trying to control the Lotus. Every capture should either:

  • Clear a path for your Lover, or
  • Remove an enemy escort from your Lotus, or
  • Remove an enemy piece that can block your Lover's next move.

Captures for their own sake — "take a free piece" — are usually wrong. They don't move you toward the Embrace.

The Endgame

The Lover reaches the Heart, but the Embrace needs two escorts. The most common losing pattern is: your Lover lands at the Heart, but you have zero or one escort in the Lotus, and the opponent has time to push a blocker into one of the cells you'd need.

So plan three pieces' arrivals together. The Lover, plus two helpers, all in formation by the same turn. The Dreamer is ideal — three cells in any direction, no capture, almost certain to reach the Lotus untouched. The Fool is great because it jumps any blockers. The Guardian (2 cells, captures by landing) can carve a slot in the Lotus by removing an enemy escort and standing on the cell.

Common Mistake

Rushing the Lover alone. She'll reach the Heart by move 6, but with no escorts in the Lotus, she stands there for ten turns while the opponent fills the Lotus with their own pieces — and you can never quite muster two friends in the cells she needs.

The Lotus Attackers

Pieces that can reach a Lotus cell in one move from various positions:

  • Dreamer — fastest. 3 cells in a line. From row 1, often reaches the Lotus in 1 move.
  • Sage — from any diagonal cell, if the path is clear.
  • Traitor — from any cell on row 3, 4, or 5, or column 3, 4, 5, if the path is clear.
  • Fool — knight jumps; ignores blockers.
  • Guardian — 2-cell range in all 8 directions; good for the final escort dash.

The Three Levers

The Lover. Move her every turn it is safe to do so. She is uncapturable but slow — 1 cell, only toward the Heart. The march matters.

The Lotus. Two of your other pieces must arrive in the 8-cell ring with her. Plan three arrivals together, not one rush.

Denial. A piece of yours in the enemy's Lotus blocks their Embrace. Sometimes a blocker is worth more than a capture.

About this Edition

PYAAR is an original 1-vs-1 strategy game inspired by chess but with its own rules, its own goal piece, and its own winning paths. The game is fully playable in a browser using only the included files — no install, no server. Open index.html to begin.

This player's manual is the literary companion to the playable prototype. The rules summary in RULES.md reflects the same content in a single page.
